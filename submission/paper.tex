\abstract{
Human subtelomeres contain duplicated sequence that is shared among the ends of
non-homologous chromosomes and provides a substrate for ectopic exchange
\cite{MeffordTrask2002,Flint1997,Linardopoulou2005,Brown1990,Wilkie1991,Trask1991}.
However, incomplete reference assemblies and chromosome-by-chromosome analyses
have prevented a population-scale view of the extent and organization of
subtelomeric exchange \cite{Nurk2022,Liao2023,hprc_hprcv2_2025}.
Here we apply a reference-free pangenome approach to 465 near-complete human
assemblies, comparing every chromosome end against every other, and find that
high-identity pseudo-homolog regions~\cite{Guarracino2023} occur on 41 of 48 chromosome arms.
These regions group into sequence communities, sets of chromosome ends that
share duplicated sequence. Across these communities, copy-number enrichment is
concentrated in gene modules associated with chromosome dynamics, intracellular
organization and germ-cell development.
Human chromosome-contact maps show preferential proximity between subtelomeres
with similar sequences, persisting even in adjacent flanks that lack the shared
sequence used to define each pair. Mouse contact maps show the same preference, and at
pair resolution the association is elevated at zygotene, when telomeres cluster at
the nuclear envelope, and at pachytene, and lowest at diplotene. In a three-generation telomere-to-telomere
pedigree, whole-genome comparison recovers the obligate X-Y crossover in PAR1, the
pseudoautosomal region shared by the X and Y chromosomes, during male meiosis, but
finds no exchange between non-homologous ends outside the acrocentric short arms and
the pseudoautosomal regions: the one apparent candidate, at the chr9q terminus, is
produced by an assembly error in a paternal haplotype, and on a corrected assembly the
transmitted haplotype matches its own homologue best. These results generalize known
subtelomeric exchange systems into a near-ubiquitous architecture whose maintenance is
ancient and recurrent, rather than detectable as individual events within a family.
}

\maketitle

Human subtelomeres are among the most rearrangement-prone and fast-evolving
regions of the human genome \cite{MeffordTrask2002}. Cytogenetic and clone-based studies established that each chromosome end is a mosaic of duplicated segments also found at the ends of several other, non-homologous chromosomes \cite{Flint1997,Linardopoulou2005}. These mosaics are organized into two domains: a proximal region of chromosome-specific sequence and a distal zone of repeats shared across many ends.
These duplicated blocks harbor subtelomeric copies of gene families and
repeat-associated loci (olfactory receptors, tubulins and the D4Z4/DUX4
macrosatellite), with copy number and sequence varying within and between human
populations
\cite{MeffordTrask2002,segdup_bailey_eichler2006,segdup_jeong_yoo2025}.
These chromosome ends also exchange sequence ectopically, between non-allelic sites (often on non-homologous chromosomes), by translocation and gene conversion \cite{Brown1990,Wilkie1991,Trask1991}, and that exchange has continued into recent human evolution, leaving much of subtelomeric sequence derived from duplications specific to the human lineage \cite{Linardopoulou2005}.

This ectopic exchange has well-known consequences at particular chromosome ends.
The pseudoautosomal regions PAR1 and PAR2 are shared by the sex chromosomes,
and PAR1 carries the obligate male sex-chromosome crossover
\cite{Rouyer1986,sexchrompars_bellott2024}.
The five acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22), whose short arms carry
ribosomal DNA, cluster around the nucleoli, where they recombine and underlie
recurrent Robertsonian translocations
\cite{acrocentric_Altemose2022,Guarracino2023,acrocentric_rdna_robertsonian_deLima2025,acrocentric_Guarracino2025ape}.
The 4q35 D4Z4 macrosatellite, homologous to the 10q26 array, is the causal
locus in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy, and the DUX4 gene it contains, normally
silenced, is reactivated in diverse cancers
\cite{dux4_d4z4_fshd_lemmers2010worldwide,dux4_cancer_Chew2019}.
Unbalanced subtelomeric rearrangements are also a recurrent cause of
intellectual disability
\cite{MeffordTrask2002}.
Yet this picture was built from heterogeneous, locus-focused evidence---fluorescence in situ hybridization, BAC and half-YAC clones, monochromosomal hybrids, and complete-assembly studies of particular chromosome classes or genomes
\cite{Mefford2001,Riethman2004,Ambrosini2007,Guarracino2023,acrocentric_rdna_robertsonian_deLima2025}---rather than a uniform map of exchange across all chromosome ends in a large haplotype-resolved cohort.

Two advances now make this view possible.
The telomere-to-telomere CHM13 assembly resolved one human genome end to end
\cite{Nurk2022}.
The second release of the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC v2)
provides near-complete, haplotype-resolved assemblies for 232 individuals from
five superpopulations \cite{hprc_hprcv2_2025}.
Our analyses of heterologous acrocentric chromosomes and complete assemblies
of human Robertsonian chromosomes established that their short arms recombine
through shared pseudo-homolog regions (PHRs)
\cite{Guarracino2023,acrocentric_rdna_robertsonian_deLima2025}. We extended this reference-free, all-to-all pangenome graph approach to the full cohort and all chromosome ends to map inter-chromosomal subtelomeric sharing without chromosomal partitioning, measure how that sharing is organized into sequence communities, and test whether those communities coincide with three-dimensional nuclear proximity and ongoing pedigree-scale recombination.
PHR calling, the sequence communities, population structure and the tests of homogenization compare
the 465 assemblies with one another, without a reference. CHM13 serves as the coordinate system of the
genome-wide survey (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}A) and the browser views (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}), and its gene
annotation is the substrate of the gene-copy enrichment (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed2}). The contact
analyses use the assembly of the genome whose contacts were measured.

\subsection*{A whole-genome survey reveals subtelomeric sharing}

We began with an unbiased, genome-wide survey of inter-chromosomal sequence sharing. We aligned 25,272 sampled
ordered pairs (11.6\% of all possible) from a 466-sequence whole-genome set
comprising 464 HPRC v2 haplotypes plus CHM13v2.0 and GRCh38.
The sample lies well above the modeled connectivity threshold for the
assembly-pair network (Methods), allowing sequence sharing to be recovered
through transitive paths even when two assemblies were not directly compared. We treated these
sampled alignments as an implicit pangenome graph: for each non-overlapping
100 kb CHM13 window we queried the indexed alignments and followed up to five
alignment hops, so a match could be reached through an intermediate assembly
(A$\rightarrow$B$\rightarrow$C) \cite{pangenome_graphs_impg_IMPG2023}.
The resulting genome-wide map shows high-identity inter-chromosomal sharing enriched at
chromosome ends (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}A). The 1.6\% of 100\,kb windows that lie within 1\,Mb of a
chromosome end hold 11\% of the windows that share sequence with another chromosome, 1.8 times the
share expected from their density of inter-chromosomal segmental duplications (permutation
$p = 0.0001$, Methods). Most shared windows lie elsewhere, in interstitial and pericentromeric
duplications. The acrocentric short
arms and pseudoautosomal regions appear as expected positive controls, and 29 of the
other 39 chromosome ends share their positional profile, with sharing highest in the windows
next to the telomere and falling inward (correlation with the control profile against circular
shifts, $p < 0.05$, Methods). Eight ends share no sequence with another chromosome above this
threshold within 5\,Mb of the telomere.

Applying this approach to 465 near-complete assemblies, we identified 15,668
PHRs on 41 of the 48 chromosome arms; the remaining seven arms (chr2p, chr3p,
chr5p, chr8q, chr11q, chr14q, chr18q) contained no PHR under our detection
criteria. We extracted the 500 kb flank inward from each telomere and aligned the
flank set in a separate, complete all-against-all comparison (not the sampled
whole-genome survey set), without restricting comparisons to homologous
chromosomes, and called a PHR where consecutive 5\,kb windows each contained at least
five alignments from each of at least two chromosomes, including the source
chromosome, at $\geq 95\%$ identity (Methods).
Walking inward from each telomere until inter-chromosomal similarity disappears,
we found that PHRs span tens to hundreds of kilobases (median 105 kb, mean 144 kb;
Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}B).
Excluding the acrocentric short arms and the pseudoautosomal regions, these PHRs
total 3.51 Mb of subtelomeric sequence, with the median PHR about a third the
length of PAR2 \cite{sexchrompars_bellott2024}.

\subsection*{Community structure of subtelomeric sharing}

To measure how much sequence chromosome ends share, we built an explicit
pangenome graph from all 15,668 PHR sequences
\cite{Garrison2024pggb,pangenome_graphs_unbiased_GarrisonGuarracino2023}. Unlike
the implicit alignment graph used for the survey, which follows sampled
alignments on demand, this graph materializes the aligned sequence structure:
shared sequence is represented by graph nodes and each PHR by a path through
those nodes. Its main connected component contains 15,654 of the sequences
(99.9\%) and is too tangled to interpret node by node.
We therefore measured pangenome similarity between chromosome ends as the Jaccard overlap of the graph nodes each traverses, and reduced the result to an arm-level similarity matrix (Methods).
The matrix is structured: locally dense blocks of high similarity are
embedded in a broader continuum of lower inter-community similarity
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2}B, left).
Leiden community detection~\cite{leiden_traag2019}, which groups a similarity
network into clusters with stronger within-cluster than between-cluster
connectivity, partitions the 41 PHR-bearing arms into 15 arm-level communities
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2}B, right).
The communities recover every previously described case of inter-chromosomal
subtelomere homology: the two pseudoautosomal pairs Xp/Yp and Xq/Yq
\cite{sexchrompars_acquaviva2020}, the five acrocentric short arms
\cite{acrocentric_Altemose2022}, the 10p/18p pair first reported by Linardopoulou
and colleagues \cite{Linardopoulou2005}, and the 4q/10q D4Z4 pair
\cite{dux4_d4z4_fshd_lemmers2010worldwide}.
Average-linkage hierarchical clustering (UPGMA) provides an alternative display and method comparison
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2}B, left), with exact agreement on 12 of the 15 communities;
we do not interpret the ordering as an evolutionary tree.
One community, C6, groups six q-arms (22q, 21q, 19q, 1q, 13q, 17q) into a broad enriched neighborhood rather than a set of discrete pairwise relationships (Methods).


At the sequence level, the representative communities shown here are
characterized by duplicons, blocks of sequence that recur at multiple
chromosome ends and may contain genes, pseudogenes or non-coding sequence
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}).
Community C1 (4q/10q) carries the D4Z4 macrosatellite and its DUX4 retrogene, with
homologous DUX4L arrays and FRG1/FRG2 paralogs on both ends (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}A)
\cite{dux4_d4z4_fshd_lemmers2010worldwide,Cabianca2012}.
The arrays vary widely between haplotypes: gene annotation transferred to each assembly
places a median of 25 DUX4/DUX4L copies, about one per D4Z4 unit, on 4q (range 2 to 82,
357 haplotypes) and 20 on 10q (range 1 to 77, 355 haplotypes). On 4q the median agrees with direct
D4Z4 counts on the complete chromosome 4 assemblies of the same release (medians of 22 to 26 repeat
units, range 7 to 133) \cite{Lucas2026}, whereas counting annotated copies gives a narrower range.
For 21 of these arrays, in seven individuals whose complete arrays were also counted on spanning
nanopore reads, the two counts agree within one unit in 17, and the read-level analysis further
resolves the 4qA, 4qB, 10qA and 10qB haplotypes and in-cis duplications of the distal subtelomere on
both arms \cite{Xiao2026}.
Community C2 (10p/18p) is anchored by a TUBB8-family duplicon, with TUBB8 on
10p and its TUBB8B paralog on 18p amid tandem tubulin-related copies
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}B) \cite{Linardopoulou2005}.
Community C11 (5q/6q) is built on the OR4F olfactory-receptor family and its
pseudogenes (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}C).
More broadly, characteristic subtelomeric duplicons, rather than community-specific protein-coding genes, define the communities
\cite{Ambrosini2007}, and subtelomeric gene content is correspondingly dominated by pseudogenes and
non-coding RNAs. Two communities consist of a single arm type: the five acrocentric short arms of
C7 (10 p--p pairs) and the six q-arms of C6 (15 q--q pairs). Across all communities, 41 of 58
within-community pairs (71\%) join two arms of the same type, against 400 of 820 (49\%) among all
pairs of the 41 PHR-bearing arms (arm type permuted with community sizes fixed, $p = 0.005$).
Without C7, C6 and the two pseudoautosomal communities, 14 of 31 within-community pairs (45\%) join
arms of the same type, against 157 of 325 (48\%) among all pairs of the remaining 26 arms
($p = 0.67$), so the other communities show no arm-type excess. The sequence immediately
inside each telomere, where that exchange is concentrated, is itself a shared and copy-variable
repeat family, which we characterise below.

\subsection*{Functional enrichment within subtelomeric PHRs}

We asked which functions are represented among the gene copies that PHRs carry,
using a copy-number-aware comparison of Gene Ontology (GO)
\cite{GeneOntologyConsortium2026} and Reactome \cite{Reactome2024} annotations
in CHM13 against all annotated gene copies elsewhere in the genome, with each
duplicated copy counted separately rather than collapsed by gene name or family
(Methods). Two features of the input bound what the comparison can say. Of the
402 gene copies whose midpoints fall in a PHR, 215 carry no GO or Reactome term
and cannot enter the test at all, over half of them uncharacterised \texttt{LOC}
loci; the result therefore rests on the 187 annotated copies. Those 187 copies
derive from only 39 gene families, and eight families contribute 142 of them.
The consequence is direct: 124 of the 209 significantly enriched terms draw every
one of their copies from a single family, and removing each term's largest contributing family
leaves none of the 209 significant. The analysis therefore describes the
abundance of a small number of amplified duplicon families, not a functional
repertoire of the compartment.

The families carrying the strongest copy-number signal fall into several
functional systems whose distributions differ markedly across the PHR communities (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed2}). The most
community-restricted system is associated with DUX4: functions related to
zygotic genome activation, transcriptional regulation, the nuclear envelope and
the cell cycle are concentrated in the community C1 exchange network linking 4q and 10q.
These terms rest on DUX4 copies alone, so their strength follows the array length of
the reference assembly: CHM13 carries 34 copies on each of 4q and 10q, above the haplotype
medians of 25 and 20, and an assembly with short arrays would carry the same terms on
fewer copies.
By contrast, functions involved in intracellular organization and chromosome
dynamics are distributed across multiple communities. A recurrent WASH/DDX11
module spans six communities, combining WASH-associated endosomal actin and
exocyst activity with DDX11-associated helicase activity and sister-chromatid
cohesion. A second distributed system is associated with male germ-cell
development and sexual reproduction. The direct GO term spermatid development
is enriched 25-fold relative to the rest of the genome and is carried by 19
SEPTIN14-associated PHR copies, 9 loci spread over 10 chromosomes from a single
family;
broader enriched terms include spermatogenesis, germ-cell development, gamete
generation and sexual reproduction. These SEPTIN14-associated copies also contribute septin and
cytokinesis functions and are concentrated in community C3, with additional copies in
communities C5, C11 and C12. Signaling functions associated with WBP1L/CXCL12 and ribosomal
and nucleolar functions associated with RPL23A are also shared among several
otherwise distinct communities.

Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed2} maps these systems across chromosome ends and
shows that PHRs do not carry a uniform functional repertoire. Instead, each
community contains a different combination of partially overlapping modules:
some are highly amplified within a single exchange network, whereas others
recur across multiple communities. This functional mosaic parallels the
underlying sequence-sharing communities: a few amplified families are distributed
among them in different combinations, rather than every chromosome end carrying a
uniform repertoire. Whether inter-chromosomal exchange redistributes these families
or whether they were amplified in place cannot be decided from presence alone.

\subsection*{Communities carry ordinary continental population structure}

If the exchange compartment were a distinct population-genetic entity, cross-arm sequence
sharing should show population differentiation unlike the rest of the genome. It does not.
Hudson $F_{ST}$ between AFR and each non-African superpopulation computed on the
community-restricted arms gives 0.10--0.15 (block-jackknife 95\% CI +0.02 to +0.24 across ten
arm/community blocks), against matched genome-wide continental values of 0.07--0.15 for the same
superpopulation labels and the same metric (ratios 0.72--1.44; AFR--EAS +0.153 versus +0.144;
AFR--EUR +0.108 versus +0.150). Within non-Africa the values are near zero or slightly negative,
as they are genome-wide. Subtelomeric sharing therefore carries the continental structure
expected for these populations rather than a subtelomere-specific signature: the compartment is
population-stratified like the rest of the genome, and exchange has not homogenised it across
populations.

The aggregate conceals how uneven that is. Across the ten arm/community blocks the AFR-versus-
non-African values span $-0.08$ to $+0.35$ (s.d.\ 0.12 over 100 arm $\times$ pair estimates):
one block is homogenised below the genome-wide level (C11/chr6q, $-0.08$; AFR--EUR $-0.25$),
several are effectively undifferentiated (C1/chr10q $+0.02$, C6/chr22q $+0.03$), and others are
as differentiated as any autosomal region (C3/chr16q $+0.35$, C15/chrXp $+0.28$). The direction
of the AFR--EUR and AFR--SAS comparisons is towards less differentiation than genome-wide, but
their jackknife intervals include the matched baseline, so we do not read that as a difference.
Nor does differentiation track sequence sharing: the block means of within-community Jaccard
distance and AFR-vs-non-African $F_{ST}$ are uncorrelated ($r = +0.07$, ten blocks), so the
block-level values are heterogeneity rather than a gradient set by exchange intensity.

\subsection*{Subtelomere similarity mirrors physical proximity}

To explore the three-dimensional configuration of these regions, we measured inter-chromosomal contact at PHR coordinates in Hi-C, Pore-C and CiFi maps. These assays quantify contact frequency between genomic regions as a proxy for nuclear proximity. Across all of them, contact between subtelomeres increases with their PHR sequence similarity.
We measured this per inter-chromosomal PHR pair within a single genome in
HG002 Pore-C (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}A; pointwise
Spearman $\rho = 0.376$, $n = 2{,}830$), with contact increasing similarly in
CHM13 Hi-C, HG002 Hi-C and HG002 CiFi (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed1}).
Because near-identical PHRs on non-homologous chromosomes cannot be told apart by
read mapping, we interpret contact at a PHR window as an aggregate over its
near-identical copies rather than reads uniquely assigned to a single PHR.
The 100\,kb immediately centromere-ward of each PHR carries no shared paralogous
sequence and is uniquely mappable, and so provides the control for this
multi-mapping concern. Two statistics on the identical arm-pair set order the two
window types oppositely. The binary contrast $B/W$ is 0.0267 in PHR windows and
0.0021 in the flanks at 50\,kb, and 0.0280 against 0.0013 at 20\,kb, which places
the unique-sequence flanks above the PHR windows; the arm-level Mantel $\rho$
between sequence similarity and contact does the opposite, 0.657 against 0.520 in
HG002 and 0.656 against 0.522 in CHM13 ($p<10^{-4}$). The disagreement is a
property of the two statistics rather than of the data: $B/W$ is a ratio of class
means and is carried by whichever term moves, so it tracks contact that crosses a
community boundary rather than the contact the partition adds (Methods). A rank correlation over
the whole arm matrix is not a two-class statistic and is insensitive to a
background common to both classes; on it the PHR windows exceed the flanks in
both haplotypes at each of the three resolutions tested, and the gap widens as bins get
finer (10\,kb: 0.705 against 0.379 in HG002, 0.717 against 0.342 in CHM13).
Both statistics are biased in the same conservative direction by multi-mapping.
Placement among $k$ equally scoring loci is uniform, so a specific contact is
attenuated by roughly $1/k$ per end, and $1/k^2$ for a PHR--PHR contact; the
attenuation is symmetric across community classes, because a misplaced read is
as likely to land between communities as within one. Misplacement therefore
drives $\rho$ down and $B/W$ up, towards the null, and the uniquely mappable
flank windows carry essentially none of it. PHR-window enrichment is thus a
lower bound, and the bound is set by the biased measurement, which already
exceeds the unbiased flank value on the rank metric. A multi-mapping artefact
predicts the reverse ordering there, PHR below flank, which is not observed.
A Pore-C contact matrix \cite{Ulahannan2019} ordered by sequence community shows
the same community block structure after observed/expected inter-chromosomal
normalization, which accounts for baseline differences in inter-chromosomal
contact so that broad nuclear-contact biases do not masquerade as community
structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}B)
\cite{hic3d_liebermanaiden2009,hic3d_imakaev2012}.
The same within-community enrichment holds in the HG002 CiFi contact map
\cite{hic3d_cifi2025}.
Subtelomeric sequence similarity predicts contact between non-homologous mouse chromosome
ends at every stage of prophase I (Spearman $\rho = 0.42$, 0.61, 0.58 and 0.25 at leptotene,
zygotene, pachytene and diplotene over 1{,}135 inter-chromosomal PHR pairs; Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}C).
Because those pairs share chromosome ends they are not independent, so we infer at pair
resolution with two-way clustered standard errors over both arms, and test the same association
against a permutation null that keeps the between-arm-pair structure: $p = 10^{-4}$ at leptotene,
zygotene and pachytene and $1.5 \times 10^{-3}$ at diplotene, with 95\% intervals of
0.37--0.47, 0.57--0.66, 0.53--0.62 and 0.19--0.30. The association is elevated at zygotene and
pachytene and lowest at diplotene. Zygotene exceeds pachytene at pair resolution (paired contrast
$-0.038$, 95\% CI $-0.067$ to $-0.009$, $p = 0.017$), but a bootstrap over the 27 chromosome ends
does not separate the two stages (95\% CI $-0.131$ to $+0.043$).
Collapsing the pairs to arm means reverses that ordering, so we do not use the arm-level
summaries for inference; they are reported in Methods only as a display.
A confound limits the interpretation: mouse p-arm flanks are 88\% satellite (mean satellite
fraction 0.885, against 0.004 on q-arms), and satellite content tracks the sequence axis at
$r = 0.79$, so in mouse the similarity that predicts contact is largely similarity of
satellite sequence. The planar contact matrices are not satellite-maskable, so satellite and
duplicon contributions cannot be separated here; we therefore read the mouse result as
proximity between similar subtelomeres in the bouquet rather than as evidence about which
sequence class drives it. We compared stage-resolved Hi-C from inbred C57BL/6 (B6) spermatocytes mapped to a combined B6 and CAST T2T reference \cite{Francis2025,Zuo2021}, holding assemblies, PHR sequence identities and read-placement
procedure constant across stages.
In mouse, where detectable PHRs are concentrated on p-arms, the per-PHR-pair
statistic peaks at zygotene for p-arm pairs. Restricting to inter-chromosomal pairs of the same
arm type, the correlation peaks at zygotene over pairs of two p-arm PHRs
(Spearman $\rho = 0.285$, 0.508, 0.323, 0.023; $n = 722$), whereas over pairs
of two q-arm PHRs it peaks at diplotene ($\rho = 0.352$, 0.338, 0.406, 0.420;
$n = 42$)---though the q-arm subset is small and p-arm windows carry broader
arm-context effects, so we do not read the arm split as a clean arm-type
experiment.

\subsection*{Non-homologous ends are homogenized, most strongly near the telomere}

High identity between non-homologous ends does not by itself show concerted evolution, because a
recent duplication leaves the same signature. Homogenization predicts more: copies at different ends
should approach allelic divergence where exchange is frequent. We compared, over every aligned base
pair of the all-against-all alignment of the 18,827 telomere-anchored windows, the divergence between
copies of the same arm in different haplotypes (allelic) with that between copies on different
chromosomes (paralogous). Paralogous copies are six times more divergent overall (median 0.89\%
against 0.15\%, Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5}b), and the ratio separates three regimes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5}a, Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:community_tiers}). Non-homologous copies are as
similar as alleles in the acrocentric short arms and PAR1 (ratio 1.0), three to four times more
divergent in C1, C11 and C3 (3.3--3.5), and 7 to 27 times in the other communities. Along the
chromosome end the median ratio is 1.2--2.3 within the first 16\,kb and 6.6 at 200--500\,kb
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5}c). A single duplication followed by independent divergence would leave the same
divergence along the whole duplicated block. A ratio that falls toward the telomere is instead
expected if exchange is most frequent at the chromosome tip.

The topology of duplicon copies gives a second test. We built trees of the copies of
24 gene families that have copies at two or more ends across the 465 haplotypes, together with the
acrocentric short-arm repeat (20 haplotypes per family, Methods, Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed8}).
Where exchange is known to occur, in the acrocentric short arms and in all seven pseudoautosomal families
(obligate crossover in PAR1), copies from different ends interleave, so the test detects exchange there.
Outside these compartments the families split. Copies of each end form their own clade in FAM87,
FRG2 and TUBB8, and in IL9R and WASIR2 apart from their pseudoautosomal copies, the pattern of an old
duplication without later exchange. No end forms its own clade in D4Z4, CIC, FAM138, OR4G, AGGF1, CLUH
and WASHC1, and only some ends do in WASH, DDX11L, SEPTIN14, OR4F and FAM157. A tree cannot date an
exchange, but interleaved copies show that some copies at different ends share a more recent ancestor
than copies at the same end.

Last, we searched for gene conversion directly, as segments of one end that carry the sequence of
another. On the copy alignments of the duplicon families, GENECONV \cite{Sawyer1989} detects two
events among TUBB8 copies, one in SEPTIN14, inside an Alu element shared with 1p and 7p, and none
in OR4F. Both TUBB8
events are supported by HiFi and ONT reads and are inherited. The maternal 18p of HG01884 ends in
38\,kb of 10p-type sequence, with the junction inside TUBB8B, and the maternal 10p of HG03239 carries
a 180\,bp tract of 18p-type sequence, and in both families the 1000 Genomes reads place the segment
in the mother (Methods). Across all windows, a scan of the alignments in 2\,kb bins finds 34,418
tracts, at 1,757 distinct positions, in which a haplotype is closer to another end than to its own
arm, and 96\% of them are shared between individuals. They cover 6.3\% of the scored sequence in C3
and 3.4\% in C11, 0.07\% in C2, whose TUBB8 copies keep to their own ends, and 9.2\% in the
acrocentric short arms. All 30 tracts we sampled are supported by HiFi reads, and 98.6\% of tracts
avoid regions flagged by assembly quality control. Conversion is visible only where the converted
segment differs from the sequence it replaced, so exchange between near-identical copies escapes
detection \cite{Mansai2010} and these counts are a lower bound. The gradient toward the telomere, the interleaved
copy trees and the inherited conversion tracts all point to repeated exchange between non-homologous
ends, rather than to duplications that have since diverged independently.

\subsection*{The exchange junction is a copy-variable TERRA promoter}

The first two kilobases inside the telomere are not ordinary subtelomeric sequence. We observe a
CpG island there (CpG observed/expected 1.02, GC 62.3\%), against 40.9\% GC at 2--5\,kb and 40.2\%
at 5--10\,kb and a genome-wide CpG observed/expected of 0.2--0.3. The window is not telomere: it
carries about one (TTAGGG)/(CCCTAA) unit per kb, where a genuine repeat array runs 150--170. Nor is
it low-complexity sequence, which is less abundant at the tip than anywhere else in the compartment
(32.8\% at 0--2\,kb against 82.4\% at 20--50\,kb, where the satellite and duplicon arrays sit). The
island is present at 88\% of ends lacking an annotated tip gene and 95\% of ends carrying one, so it
is not a by-product of gene annotation.

This is a known element. The human terminal repeat region is built, centromere to telomere, from a
61\,bp, a 29\,bp and a 37\,bp tandem repeat, and the 29\,bp and 37\,bp elements together form a DNA
island rich in CpG dinucleotides that functions as the promoter driving TERRA
\cite{Brown1990,Nergadze2009,Farnung2010}; the 29\,bp and 37\,bp elements alone suffice to drive a
reporter, TERRA start sites on XqYq lie 27\,nt downstream of the last 37\,bp repeat and about
250\,bp upstream of the telomeric tract \cite{Nergadze2009}, and the element is bound in vivo by
active RNA polymerase II \cite{Nergadze2009} and sits next to a CTCF/cohesin site within 1--2\,kb of
the TTAGGG tract \cite{Deng2012}. In T2T-CHM13v2.0, 39 of 46 subtelomeres carry a TERRA promoter,
classed by repeat organisation \cite{Santagostino2025}, and an older 3.6\,kb repeat immediately
adjacent to the tract likewise contains a conserved CpG-rich region whose copy number varies among
telomeres \cite{Cheng1991}. Our measurement therefore confirms rather than discovers: the CpG island
at the exchange junction is the CpG-island class of TERRA promoter, and its GC content sits at the
upper extreme of a subtelomeric range already known to run from 62.5\% at 1p to 37.5\% at 3p against
a genome-wide 41.6\% \cite{Riethman2004}.

The genes annotated at the tip are a separate and weaker matter. Seven of 46 ends have an annotated
transcription start site within 2\,kb of the tract and 14 of 46 within 5\,kb; the DDX11L family
supplies 8 of the 15 genes at 1.7--2.5\,kb, with LOC102723376, LINC01986 (749\,bp) and LINC02684
(859\,bp) the only non-DDX11L loci inside 1\,kb. Their presence does not explain the exchange
regime: arms with a tip gene have a median paralogous/allelic divergence ratio of 3.06 against 3.82
for arms without one (Mann--Whitney $p = 0.85$). Whatever sets how homogenized an arm is, it is not
which gene happens to be annotated at its end.

The promoter is also why the terminal few kilobases cannot be measured by sequence comparison, which
is a stronger statement than irreproducibility. Same-arm alignments collapse there: 41--60 records of
median block 2,728--3,007\,bp in the 2--4\,kb bin, against 4,933--5,283 records of about
50,000\,bp in the 28--30\,kb bin. A tandem array whose copy number and phase differ between
haplotypes produces exactly that---short, phase-shifted, partly paralogous matches instead of one
homologous stretch. The paralogous/allelic ratio in that zone is therefore undefined rather than
noisy, and we exclude it rather than report it (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed6}). Outside the
excluded zone the ratio behaves regularly: about 1.3 within the first 8\,kb, rising through 2--3 at
20--60\,kb to 6--7 beyond 300\,kb.

\subsection*{Direct test for exchange between non-homologous ends}

We traced transmission through the WashU pedigree \cite{Cechova2025}, the only one with complete
telomere-to-telomere assemblies in all three generations (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed7}A), to ask whether a
recent exchange between non-homologous ends can be detected within a single family. Father-to-daughter
transmission is a favourable setting, because recombination is biased toward chromosome ends in the male
germline \cite{Bherer2017}, and the same procedure recovers PAR1 sharing between Xp and Yp, the
obligate site of male sex-chromosome recombination.

An initial pass flagged one candidate: the terminus of the daughter's paternal chr9q, whose best
whole-genome match appeared to be her father's chr3q while the match to his own chr9q fell away
(Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed7}B,C). Complete evaluation shows that this call is an artefact of the father's
assembly at that locus. In both assembly releases the terminal ~150\,kb of his chr9q haplotype 2
disagrees with his own long reads, and the release quality control marks a misjoin at the start of the copied block.
Against the daughter's sequence in the called tract the released haplotype reaches IMPG dice similarity 0.9918,
the corrected haplotype 1.0000.
The error is what created the interchromosomal winner: the released assembly carries, at that terminus,
a reverse-complement copy of a segment of his own chr1p (100\% of the segment's canonical 21-mers fall
within chr1p, collinear over 60\,kb at constant offset), so his own chr9q haplotype was absent from that
region and the closest remaining sequence, his chr3q, won by default. The inserted chr1p segment is
itself a member of the family shared with chr3q, which is why the same block reads as both chr1-like and
chr3-like.

We therefore repeated the comparison with all candidate ends offered to the similarity computation
simultaneously rather than one alignment setting at a time, on the assemblies we corrected with each
individual's own reads (Methods), in which the father's chr9q terminus comes from his own high-fidelity
reads alone, without any data from the daughter. On those assemblies the daughter's
paternal chr9q matches her father's chr9q at IMPG dice similarity 1.0000 across the called tract, and his chr3q is
second at 0.9985. The chr3-like character of the terminus is therefore real, but it
is similarity between shared subtelomeric blocks rather than a donor relationship: a 0.15\%
difference in dice, well inside the range we measure at chromosome ends generally, where the
median end's closest non-homologous partner is 0.52\% divergent (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed5}e).
His other chr9q haplotype does not carry this sequence at all: the 20\,kb tract has no alignment within
the last 3\,Mb of that haplotype. That the father's chr3q terminus matches his daughter's chr9q at 0.9985
while one of his own chr9q copies lacks the sequence is the concerted-evolution signal itself: a
non-homologous end carries a block that a homologous end does not.

The chr1p/chr5q similarity visible in the same scans is likewise an artefact, of the alignment rather
than of the assembly. At the seed-frequency cutoff used for the whole-genome scan the father's chr5q
candidate never enters the alignment, so chr1p wins by default; at higher sensitivity the chr5q
homologue reaches the last base of its contig at identity 1.0000 and matches the daughter's tract at 48
diagnostic sites against 1 for chr1p (Methods).

Neither run remains when we repeat the whole-genome comparison on the assemblies of all four
individuals corrected with their own reads (Methods). From father to daughter the only runs between
non-homologous chromosomes are PAR1 (138\,kb) and acrocentric short arms (47 runs, 830\,kb), and
two thin runs from the interior of chr1 and chr3 to acrocentric short arms (Extended Data
Fig.~\ref{fig:ed7}C). From mother to daughter (9 runs, 182\,kb) and from daughter to granddaughter
(22 runs, 396\,kb) every such run joins acrocentric short arms.

We conclude that this pedigree contains \textbf{no evidence of exchange between non-homologous ends
outside the acrocentric short arms and the pseudoautosomal regions}. Within those two compartments such sharing
is expected: PAR1 crosses over in every male meiosis, and the acrocentric short arms exchange sequence,
including ribosomal DNA arrays, between non-homologous chromosomes \cite{Guarracino2023}. They are accordingly the only regions in which non-homologous copies
are as similar as alleles (ratio 1.00 and 0.99; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:community_tiers}). Acrocentric-acrocentric sharing
appears in both germlines, consistent with the rDNA exchange system \cite{Guarracino2023}. Everywhere
else the sharing we detect is family-level similarity between blocks that have been exchanging on a
timescale far longer than a pedigree can observe, not a discrete event.
With no event outside these compartments in the three transmissions we examined, the pedigree
implies no rate of discrete exchange that would conflict with the four discrete 4q/10q transfers
reported across human populations \cite{dux4_d4z4_fshd_lemmers2010worldwide}.

\subsection*{Discussion}

Together, these results define a subtelomeric exchange compartment in the human pangenome, a distributed
set of chromosome ends that share high-identity sequence, sit in preferential
three-dimensional proximity, and recurrently exchange material. Individual pieces
of this compartment have long been recognized through cytogenetics and locus-specific studies,
but had not been assembled into a systematic, population-scale view across nearly
complete human genomes. The HPRC v2 assemblies let us build one, turning scattered
observations into a sequence-resolved map across 41 chromosome arms, in which the
pseudoautosomal, acrocentric, 10p/18p and 4q/10q systems stand out as local peaks
within a broader continuum.
The shared sequence itself has a consistent internal architecture
(Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed3}). Across the 46 arm ends and
9.6 Gbp of inter-arm alignment in the HPRCv2 all-vs-all maps, 98.6\% of matched base pairs
sit in the same orientation relative to the telomere (98.4\% for p--q pairs, whose coordinate
systems run in opposite directions), yet the order in which those modules lie along the arm is
not conserved: order concordance across shared partners is negligible, and it does not improve
within communities. What does track community membership is the module repertoire
(Jaccard 0.83 within versus 0.57 between communities, permutation $p = 5\times10^{-10}$) and
the length of the shared blocks (median 13.3 versus 9.9 kb). The inverted exceptions are few: the
arm pairs that are net inverted are built from thousands of small blocks rather than one
inverted segment. The pattern expected for a compartment maintained by repeated duplication and
orientation-biased conversion is therefore not a conserved arrangement of a fixed set of
blocks, but a shared module repertoire laid out in a consistent telomere-to-centromere
orientation and reassorted freely in between. The 3.51 Mb of subtelomeric sequence mapped here
traces the routes along which chromosome-end sequence has repeatedly been copied,
exchanged and homogenized \cite{Dover1982,concerted_evolution_nahr_Hastings2009}.

This compartment is also internally organized. Only two communities are built from a single arm
type: the acrocentric short arms of C7, which carry the ribosomal DNA arrays, and the six q-arms of
C6, a softly bounded neighborhood whose basis is not yet clear. Their shared modules are
orientation-consistent relative to the telomere while freely reassorted in order
(Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed3}). The broader pattern is consistent with a simple,
self-reinforcing model: telomere clustering at the meiotic nuclear envelope brings similar ends
into a shared compartment, and the association between sequence similarity and contact is
elevated at zygotene, when that clustering occurs, and at pachytene; proximity may then favour ectopic
recombination that helps keep those sequences homogenized. Shared sequence and shared function then feed back to
promote co-localization, so that sequence, function and three-dimensional
proximity continually reinforce one another. The acrocentric short arms show
the loop most cleanly: their shared rDNA draws them to the nucleolus, and
nucleolar contact drives the ectopic recombination that homogenizes them
\cite{Guarracino2023}. We see the same logic operating on other functional
modules outside the nucleolus organizer regions.

The element at the junction sharpens this picture in one respect and limits it in another. The
CpG-island TERRA promoter is not a unique sequence that happens to lie near a telomere: it is a
dispersed repeat family present, in different tandem copy numbers and arrangements, at most
chromosome ends \cite{Brown1990,Nergadze2009,Santagostino2025}. The immediate substrate of inter-arm
exchange is therefore itself a shared, copy-variable repeat---the same kind of duplicon that the rest
of the compartment is built from, and one whose variation between haplotypes is already documented.
That makes it a plausible participant in the exchange we quantify, although it does not demonstrate
that exchange occurs within it.

The same element bounds what the divergence profile can say. Because copy number and phase of the
promoter array differ between haplotypes, same-arm alignments inside the first few kilobases are
fragmentary and partly paralogous, and no orthologous comparison survives there. We therefore do not
extrapolate the ratio to the telomere itself and do not claim that it approaches one at the junction;
the measured statement is that, one vote per chromosome arm, paralogues are about 1.8-fold more
diverged than alleles just outside the excluded zone, against 6.6-fold in the far interior, so the
terminal sequence is the most nearly interchangeable we can measure and the promoter array is where
that measurement stops.

It is worth being precise about what such a ratio is, because it is easy to read it as a rate of
events and it is not one. In PAR1 a crossover is obligate in every male meiosis, and yet two copies there
still coalesce ~62,500 generations ago, as do copies on different acrocentric short arms, because
exchange does not make a genealogy shallow, it makes both loci a single pool whose genealogy is as deep
as the population's. What the data constrain is therefore a ratio of genealogical
depths, $R$, and not an interval between exchanges: the average block's common ancestor is 5.9 times
older than that of two alleles, the far interior 6.6 times, the telomeric end of the compartment 1.8
times, and where mixing is complete $R = 1$ (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:exchange_regimes}). Mixing relative to complete
mixing follows as $1/(R-1)$, running from 1.2 at the telomeric end to 0.02 in the weakest blocks. $R$ is
the robust quantity: it is a ratio of two divergences measured identically in the same compartment, so
the substitution rate cancels, whereas the absolute times inherit the uncertainty from GC-biased gene
conversion in this GC-rich sequence and from the elevated paternal-germline rate.

This concerted process operates at a characteristic scale. The exchanged blocks
are on the order of tens to hundreds of kilobases, large enough to carry an intact functional
module, but small
enough to leave chromosome identity intact rather than translocating whole
arms. We used copy-number-aware ontology enrichment as a descriptive way to
summarize which annotated gene modules are disproportionately represented in
the PHR compartment. Because duplicated copies are counted separately, these
results describe annotation burden across physical gene copies rather than
independent functional systems. The copy-number-aware enrichment pattern
reflects this block-scale organization, and the systems that resolve all map
onto chromosome inheritance across generations: sister-chromatid cohesion and
chromosome organization, germ-cell development, and genome reactivation after
fertilization. The dispersal of multiple related copies across chromosome ends
could buffer against loss of any one copy, while recurrent exchange could both
maintain similarity and generate new variants
\cite{Linardopoulou2005,Ambrosini2007}. PHRs may therefore provide both
a stable foundation for chromosome transmission and the flexibility needed for
germline systems to accommodate continuing change in chromosome-end sequence and
organization.

One mechanism we can exclude is a role for the genes at the tip. Whether an arm carries an annotated
subtelomeric gene within 5\,kb of its tract---overwhelmingly a DDX11L pseudogene---has no bearing on
how homogenized that arm is ($p = 0.85$). The homogenization is a property of the shared repeat
architecture, not of the gene complement annotated alongside it.
Telomere length is also specific to each chromosome end and conserved across individuals
\cite{Karimian2024}. The telomere tracts assembled next to our 18,827 windows reproduce the shortest
ends of that study, 12p and 17p ranking first and fourth of 39 ends without the acrocentric short
arms and the sex chromosomes, but not its longest, with 4q, 12q and 3p ranking 23rd to 32nd. Our
telomeres come from lymphoblastoid cell lines, whose telomeres lengthen after immortalization
\cite{Lucas2026}, rather than from blood, which may explain the difference. Median
telomere length per end correlates only weakly with how homogenized the end is (Spearman
$\rho = 0.34$ over 36 such ends, permutation $p = 0.04$, longer telomeres at less homogenized ends,
and $\rho = 0.03$ when the acrocentric and sex-chromosome ends are included). End-specific telomere
length and subtelomeric exchange are therefore at most weakly related.

Exchange between non-homologous chromosome ends is not peculiar to humans. In chimpanzee and gorilla,
subterminal caps of a 32-bp satellite interspersed with hyperexpanded segmental duplications sit at 42
and 80 of 96 chromosome tips, respectively \cite{Ventura2012}, and complete ape assemblies resolve them
as multi-megabase structures that evolved independently in each species \cite{Yoo2025}. In chimpanzee
spermatocytes, non-homologous chromosome termini remain associated after the bouquet stage in 32\% of
pachytene cells, which has been proposed as the setting for ectopic pairing and concerted evolution of
these blocks \cite{Hirai2019}. In common bean, phylogenetically related units of the subtelomeric khipu
satellite are spread across many non-homologous ends \cite{Richard2013}, and a subtelomeric repeat
specific to one gene pool reached most terminal knobs within 0.165 million years \cite{Chen2018}. In
yeasts, gene families with subtelomeric members expand much faster than other families \cite{Brown2010}.
In \emph{Plasmodium falciparum}, var genes recombine between subtelomeres of heterologous chromosomes
that cluster at the nuclear periphery \cite{FreitasJunior2000}, and most of this recombination happens
during mitosis \cite{Claessens2014}. Across 117 vertebrate genomes, segmental duplications near the
chromosome ends of birds and mammals are biased toward copies on other chromosomes, the reverse of the
genome-wide pattern \cite{Aqil2026}. These systems differ in what they exchange, satellites or gene
families, and in when, at meiosis or at mitosis. Where nuclear organization has been examined, the ends
that share sequence also meet, in peripheral clusters in \emph{Plasmodium} and in meiotic associations of
non-homologous ends in chimpanzee and common bean \cite{FreitasJunior2000,Hirai2019,Richard2013}. The human
compartment we describe is one instance of this widespread arrangement, resolved here across complete
haplotypes from a population.

Which factor initiates the loop---sequence similarity, shared function, or physical proximity---remains unresolved, and the acrocentric example, where rDNA both drives and results from nucleolar clustering, suggests there may be no single starting point. The current evidence has gaps: human contact maps are somatic rather than meiotic, and the meiotic-stage data come only from mouse. Improved evidence may shed light on these questions: germline-stage contact maps and larger multigenerational pedigrees with complete, haplotype-resolved telomere-to-telomere assemblies \cite{Cechova2025}, together with orthogonal assays such as FISH \cite{Trask1991}. By applying reference-free pangenome methods to the 465-assembly HPRC v2 panel, we convert relationships inferred from cytogenetics decades ago into a population-scale view of concerted evolution in human subtelomeres.

\subsection*{Whole-genome implicit pangenome survey}

The dataset comprised 464 haplotype-resolved HPRC v2 assemblies from 232
individuals plus CHM13v2.0, for 465 near-complete assemblies, with
superpopulation labels assigned at the HPRC individual level
\cite{hprc_hprcv2_2025,Nurk2022}. For the whole-genome survey we
added GRCh38, giving 466 input sequences, and used wfmash v0.23.0~\cite{wfmash}
(\texttt{-p 95 -P inf}) to align 25,272 sampled ordered target--query
pairs, representing 11.6\% of the $466^{2}$ possible ordered pairs.
We set this sample size using the Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi random-graph model.
Under that model the network of 466 sequences is connected with near-certainty
(probability above 99.99\%) once about 3.5\% of the possible pairs are drawn. We sampled more than
this so that queries need few transitive hops, not only a connected network. Because alignment is symmetric, sampling 11.6\% of the ordered pairs connects about 22\% of the unordered sequence pairs, which exceeds the model's diameter-two threshold for this graph ($\sqrt{2\ln(466)/466}\approx 16\%$); any two sequences are therefore expected to be linked through at most one intermediate. We indexed the resulting pairwise alignments with IMPG v0.4.1. For Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}A, we queried the implicit pangenome graph in
non-overlapping 100 kb windows spanning CHM13, following at most five transitive
alignment hops at a minimum estimated identity of 98\%, and summarized for each
window the distinct chromosome origins and mean identity of the sequences reached
\cite{pangenome_graphs_impg_IMPG2023}.
To test whether this sharing is concentrated at chromosome ends, we called a window shared when at
least one chromosome other than its own was reached by at least 47 haplotypes (10\%), and asked
what share of the 1,132 shared windows lies within 1\,Mb of a chromosome end (124, 11.0\%, against
504 of 31,185 windows, 1.6\%). Because segmental duplications concentrate in subtelomeres, we
permuted the shared label 10,000 times among windows of the same inter-chromosomal
segmental-duplication density class (CHM13v2.0 duplication annotation, partner on another
chromosome; covered fraction below 0.001, 0.001--0.1, 0.1--0.25, 0.25--0.5, 0.5--0.75 and 0.75--1). The
expected share at the ends is 6.1\% (95\% interval 5.1--7.1\%), and with classes of any
duplication it is 4.0\%.
To compare positional profiles, we took for each chromosome end the number of other chromosomes
reached by at least 47 haplotypes in each 100\,kb window over the 5\,Mb next to the telomere, and
correlated it with the mean profile of the acrocentric short arms and the two X pseudoautosomal
ends (the CHM13v2.0 Y has PAR1 masked). For each end the null distribution is the correlation after
circularly shifting its own profile by 1 to 49 windows, which keeps its autocorrelation and breaks
its alignment to the telomere.

\subsection*{Exclusions and flank extraction}

GRCh38 lacks complete subtelomeres and the CHM13v2.0 Y chromosome has its PAR1
masked, leaving neither with telomere-anchored ends, so we excluded both from
flank extraction. We detected telomeres with \texttt{seqtk} 1.4-r130
(\texttt{telo})~\cite{seqtk} and classified each contig at least 1 Mb long by the
chromosome arm it spans, its p-arm, its q-arm, or both arms when it crosses the
centromere. At every telomere-bearing end we trimmed the telomeric repeat and
retained the 500 kb immediately internal to it as the flank, so a
centromere-spanning contig yields both a p-arm and a q-arm flank, producing
18,827 candidate flanks across the 48 arms. We then excluded the chr18q flank of
NA18982 haplotype 1 (contig JBKABS010000018.1) as a scaffolding chimera, since in
the whole-genome wfmash alignments its first 83.37 Mb map to chr18 and a terminal
966 kb (83.38--84.35 Mb) map to chrX PAR1, joined across a 100 bp gap of Ns,
leaving 18,826 flanks for the all-against-all alignment and PHR calling.

\subsection*{Alignment, implicit pangenome graph and PHR detection}

We aligned all 18,826 flank sequences all-against-all with wfmash
v0.23.0~\cite{wfmash} at 95\% minimum identity. Every flank was therefore
compared with every other without restricting comparisons to homologous
chromosomes. We indexed this pairwise alignment set with IMPG v0.4.1 for direct
interval queries \cite{pangenome_graphs_impg_IMPG2023}. Starting at each
telomere, we queried 5 kb windows in 5 kb steps, stopping after four consecutive
windows failed the detection criteria. A window passed when the direct IMPG
query returned at least five alignments from each of at least two
chromosomes, including its own (so pseudoautosomal Xp/Yp and Xq/Yq, with a
single partner, are also detected), at a minimum identity of 95\% and minimum
output length of 3 kb.
This defined 15,668 PHRs from the 18,826 retained flanks (83.2\%).
The arm-level result does not depend on the stopping rule or on the scan length. Stopping after one,
two or three failed windows gives 15,624, 15,664 and 15,668 PHRs, and limiting the inward scan to
25--400\,kb gives 15,668, all on the same 41 arms. Requiring 3, 6 or 15 chromosomes per window instead
of 2 leaves 40, 38 and 37 arms (14,369, 13,422 and 10,030 PHRs). The 95\% identity, the
five-alignment minimum and the 5\,kb window are fixed in the stored alignment index and were not
varied.

\subsection*{The terminal CpG island, GC content and annotatability}

We characterized the sequence immediately inside each telomere on the same telomere-first 500\,kb
flanks used for PHR calling, cut into 2\,kb bins measured from the junction. Each bin was scored for
GC content and for CpG observed/expected (the count of CpG dinucleotides divided by the count
expected from the bin's own G and C frequencies), and a CpG island was called where observed/expected
exceeded 0.6 and GC exceeded 55\% simultaneously; both thresholds were fixed before the distance
profile was inspected, and genome-wide CpG observed/expected in these assemblies is 0.2--0.3, which
leaves an order of magnitude of headroom between ordinary sequence and an island. To establish that
the tip windows are subtelomeric rather than telomeric we counted (TTAGGG)+(CCCTAA) units per kb in
the same bins; to exclude low-complexity sequence as the cause of any anomaly we computed the
fraction of each bin occupied by 6-mers that recur within that bin. Statistics were taken over
30--120 sampled telomere-anchored windows per measurement (30 for CpG observed/expected, GC and low
complexity; 40 for telomeric repeat content). Gene transcription start sites were taken from the
CHM13v2.0 RefSeq/Liftoff v5.2 annotation and reduced to one distance per chromosome end: the gap
between the telomere-proximal end of the flank and the nearest annotated start site. Alignability was
measured on same-arm alignment records per 2\,kb bin, assigning a record to a bin only when the
midpoint of both the query and the target span fell in that bin, exactly as in the distance profile;
we report the number of records and the median block length. Bins whose allelic divergence exceeds
0.4\%, four times the interior baseline, are discarded from the distance profile because the allelic
denominator has failed there; the hatched zone of Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed6} is generated from
that rule and not placed by hand.

\subsection*{Tests of homogenization}

\emph{Allelic and paralogous divergence.} From the all-against-all alignments of the telomere-anchored
windows (wfmash, with =/X operations in the CIGAR), the divergence of a record is the fraction of
mismatched aligned positions. Allelic records join windows of the same arm in different haplotypes,
and paralogous records join windows on different chromosomes. For each arm pair we took the median,
weighted by aligned base pairs, over records with at least 2\,kb aligned (Extended Data
Table~\ref{tab:community_tiers}), and Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5}b weights every aligned base pair. For
Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5}c we assigned each record to a distance bin only when the midpoints of its query and
target spans lie in the same bin (2\,kb bins to 50\,kb, coarser beyond), divided the median paralogous
by the median allelic divergence per arm and bin, and plotted the median over arms.

\emph{Duplicon trees.} We intersected the Liftoff gene annotation of each assembly with the windows and
grouped genes into families by name, merging numbered paralogues and pseudogenes of one family. Of
130 families with copies on at least two arms in at least 10\% of the haplotypes, we tested 30: the
named families with a median copy longer than 1\,kb, plus OR4F and OR4G, with DUX4 and DUX4L tested
together as the D4Z4 repeat unit. For each family we cut a
reference of up to 4\,kb from its CHM13 copy, found copies in the windows of the family's arms with
FastGA (\texttt{-l500 -i0.7 -f8192}), kept copies aligned at $\geq 80$\% identity (at most ten evenly
spaced copies per window) in 20 haplotypes sampled across superpopulations, projected them onto the
reference columns, and built neighbour-joining trees from p-distances with gaps treated as missing.
An arm forms its own clade when all its copies fall in one clade of the tree. Six families had copies
on one arm only and were not drawn. As a control, the same procedure on the adjacent 100\,kb of unique
sequence recovers each individual's two haplotypes as sister copies for all 124 arms tested.

\emph{Gene conversion.} We ran GENECONV 1.81a \cite{Sawyer1989} (\texttt{/w123 /lp /es}) on the copy
alignments of TUBB8, OR4F, SEPTIN14 and D4Z4 across all haplotypes in batches of 20 haplotypes, after
removing copies with less than 90\% of columns or more than 2\% divergence from
the consensus of their own arm. We counted a copy as converted when it appeared in at least three
fragments between arms with simulated $p < 0.05$ divided by the number of batches. The consensus
sequences of the 4q and 10q D4Z4 units are indistinguishable, so conversion between them cannot be
assigned this way. We checked
candidates with the HPRC HiFi and ONT read alignments to the same assemblies and traced them in the
1000 Genomes high-coverage reads of the parents with canonical 31-mers specific to the converted
segment and with read pairs joining the segment to its flanks. For the genome-wide scan, each window's
alignments were cut into 2\,kb bins, and for every other haplotype we kept the record covering most of
the bin (at least 75\%). A bin is converted when it has at least 20 allelic copies and the median
divergence to the copies of some other arm, with at least 20 copies, is lower than the median allelic
divergence by at least 0.25\%. The scan follows the principle that sites at which a copy carries the
allele of another locus rather than its own, clustered along the sequence, mark converted tracts
\cite{Harpak2017}, but compares each haplotype with the other haplotypes of its own arm and of every
other arm instead of with orthologs in other species. Runs of converted bins form tracts, and we kept tracts flanked on both
sides by bins that match the window's own arm. Tracts at overlapping distance from the telomere with
the same donor arm in different individuals were merged into positions and counted as shared. We
compared tracts with HMM-Flagger v1.2.1 calls of the HPRC release and inspected HiFi reads at 20
tracts found in one individual and 10 shared tracts.

\emph{Telomere length.} We took the telomere tract that \texttt{seqtk telo} called next to each of
the 18,827 windows (all windows have one), and per chromosome end the median over haplotypes. The
assemblies come from lymphoblastoid cell lines, whose telomeres differ from those of blood
\cite{Lucas2026}. We correlated the median with the ratio between the divergence to the closest arm
on another chromosome and the allelic divergence of the end (Spearman, 10,000 permutations).

\subsection*{Pangenome graph, similarity and community detection}

We extracted the 15,668 called PHR intervals and supplied those sequences as the
input to pggb v0.7.4 (commit 498c5d7, with wfmash v0.23.0, seqwish v0.7.11, smoothxg v0.8.2, odgi v0.9.2, GFAffix v0.2.1 and vg v1.62.0) at 95\% identity, giving an explicit graph with 477,726 nodes and 786,318 edges. We computed all-pairs length-weighted Jaccard similarity from this graph with odgi, giving a $15{,}668 \times
15{,}668$ matrix
\cite{Garrison2024pggb,pangenome_graphs_odgi_GuarracinoHeumos2022,andreace2023pangenome,heumos2024nfcore}.
We computed a 2D layout of the main connected component with odgi and rendered it
in R (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2}A), each node colored by the dominant arm-level Leiden
community of the PHR paths crossing it. The arm-level distance matrix is the mean
pairwise Jaccard distance between PHR sequences within each arm pair. From it, arm-level
communities were inferred with the Leiden algorithm (R, igraph) after converting distances to graph weights
$w_{ij} = \exp(-d_{ij} / \mathrm{median}(d))$ and scanning Leiden resolution from
0.1 to 3.0; we selected the maximum-silhouette partition, 15 communities at
resolution 1.16, and the same community count held across resolutions 1.13--1.18.
We classified every pair of arms assigned to the same community as either
same-type (p--p or q--q) or mixed (p--q) and compared the
within-community counts with the corresponding counts across all
$\binom{41}{2}=820$ possible pairs of PHR-bearing arms.
We tested the excess of same-type pairs by permuting arm type across arms with community sizes
fixed (20,000 permutations, one-sided), once over all 41 arms and once without the acrocentric
community C7, the pseudoautosomal communities C14 and C15, and C6 (26 arms, 325 pairs).

\subsection*{Copy-number-aware gene ontology enrichment}

We counted gene copies from the CHM13v2.0 RefSeq/Liftoff v5.2 annotation
(61,312 gene records), treating every genomic copy as a separate unit: copies
sharing a gene name or family were kept distinct, so a gene present at 20
positions counted as 20 copies rather than one collapsed family entry. Before
looking at any PHR coordinate, we decided which gene's annotation each copy was
allowed to carry: a copy used its own annotation, or, if it had none of its own,
inherited terms only through a single explicitly directed NCBI Gene ``related
functional gene'' link; copies with ambiguous, type-only or unsupported evidence
were left unannotated and contributed no terms. This kept the number of genomic
copies separate from the source used to annotate them.

The test universe is therefore smaller than the number of copies: 402 gene copies
have midpoints inside PHRs, and 187 of them carry at least one usable term, the
remaining 215 having no GO or Reactome assignment (52\% of those are
uncharacterised \texttt{LOC} loci). K = 187 was tested across 31{,}235
term-by-set combinations, with the copy-number-aware background described above,
and 209 terms passed; 124 of those 209 draw every copy from a single gene family,
which is reported alongside each term so that family-driven results are not read
as compartment-wide functional statements.

Direct Gene Ontology assignments came from the NCBI human gene2go snapshot of 13
July 2026, propagated through the go-basic release of 15 June 2026; Reactome
direct assignments and their parent pathways came from release 96 (1 April
2026), and the NCBI Gene registry snapshots were dated 16 July 2026
\cite{GeneOntologyAshburner2000,GeneOntologyConsortium2026,Reactome2024}. We
tested each copy's directly assigned terms and, separately, the broader parent
terms above them in the ontology hierarchy, and fixed the full set of 31,235
database, annotation-level and term combinations before splitting copies into
PHR and non-PHR, so that no term was chosen after seeing its result.

A copy entered the analysis if it carried at least one GO or Reactome
assignment, giving a fixed universe of $N=31{,}966$ copies. We called a gene a
PHR gene when its annotation midpoint fell inside a CHM13 PHR, which placed
$K=187$ copies in PHRs and $31{,}779$ outside; a further 215 of the 402 copies
with midpoints in PHRs had no confident annotation, and we kept them as a
coverage limitation. The rest of the genome was the background, with no
sampling, permutation or spatial matching. For each term we compared the number
of PHR copies carrying it against the genome-wide count with the exact one-sided
hypergeometric upper tail over this fixed population (size $N$, $K$ PHR copies,
and the genome-wide count of term-bearing copies), an exact finite-population
test of term-bearing physical-copy excess. Because many PHR copies are
near-identical paralogs from the same amplified family, they are not independent
biological replicates; the $p$-value quantifies evidence for copy-burden excess,
not the number of independent functional systems. As the effect size we report
the PHR and non-PHR copy fractions, the fold enrichment and odds ratio (both raw
and with 0.5 added to each cell, the Haldane--Anscombe correction, so a term
absent from the background still gives a finite value), and the
observed-minus-expected PHR copy burden.

We controlled multiple testing with Benjamini--Hochberg within each of the four
annotation families (GO biological process, molecular function, cellular
component, and Reactome) and with Holm across all 31,235 tests, calling a term
enriched when its within-family $q\leq0.05$ and global Holm-adjusted
$P\leq0.05$. We used an any-overlap definition of a PHR gene only to check
sensitivity to PHR boundaries. After the tests were fixed, we mapped the
contributing copies back to the 15 arm communities and grouped related terms
into the six functional classes shown in Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed2}.

\subsection*{Hierarchical clustering and resampling controls}

For display and method comparison we also ordered the arm-level matrix by
average-linkage hierarchical clustering (UPGMA) with \texttt{hclust} (R \texttt{stats}); cutting the dendrogram into 14 clusters gave
a mean silhouette of 0.342 (\texttt{silhouette}, R \texttt{cluster} package) and exact agreement with 12 of the 15 Leiden
communities, and we use this dendrogram as a heatmap ordering, not as a
phylogenetic model. To assess the sharpness of the C6 boundary, we
bootstrap-resampled the PHRs and rebuilt the arm-level trees (R package ape).
The exact six-arm grouping was unstable under this coarse resampling,
consistent with C6 representing a broad, softly bounded neighborhood in the
similarity matrix. Because this resampling does not reconstruct the full
15,668-sequence Jaccard matrix, it is not a test of the Leiden partition
itself.

\subsection*{Hi-C, Pore-C and CiFi pipeline}

All human contact maps come from somatic cell lines, none from germ cells. The HG002 Hi-C, Pore-C
and CiFi data come from the lymphoblastoid cell line GM24385 (NA24385), the other HPRC samples from
lymphoblastoid cell lines, and CHM13 Hi-C from the CHM13hTERT cell line, derived from a complete
hydatidiform mole \cite{Nurk2022}. The mouse contact maps come from spermatocytes at successive
meiotic stages \cite{Zuo2021}.
We reprocessed Hi-C, Pore-C and CiFi from raw reads
against the corresponding T2T reference (CHM13v2.0 or the matched HPRC v2
haplotype), keeping multi-mapping reads. Standard pipelines apply a
mapping-quality filter that drops multi-mapping reads, but subtelomeric PHRs are
so similar across chromosomes that their reads map to several places at once, so
this filter removes exactly the signal we analyze. We processed Pore-C (NlaIII) and CiFi (DpnII) with pore-c-py 2.1.5, minimap2 2.30 and pairtools 1.1.3, and Hi-C with HiC-Pro 3.0.0 and
Bowtie2 2.5.3, binning contacts with cooler 0.10.4 and samtools 1.23 under Python 3.12.12 (numpy 1.26.4, pandas 3.0.0, scipy 1.17.0). Because we apply no mapping-quality filter, a read mapping equally well to
several paralogous ends is kept and placed at one of them at random rather than
discarded. PHR-internal contacts are therefore aggregate signal over these
near-identical ends, not reads confidently assigned to a single end. We generated contact matrices at 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 kb resolution.
For the per-PHR-pair analyses (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}A, Extended Data
Fig.~\ref{fig:ed1} and Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}C) we summed the balanced contacts over
the valid bin-pairs spanning the two PHR windows and divided by the number of
valid bin-pairs, giving the mean contact per bin-pair. This normalization removes
differences in contact opportunity caused by different numbers of valid bins, so
pairs spanning more bins do not automatically have larger total contact.

We formed pairs within one genome, because a contact map holds only its own sample. In HG002 the
77 PHR sequences on 41 arms give $\binom{77}{2} = 2{,}926$ pairs, and removing the 96 pairs on the
same chromosome leaves the 2,830 pairs of Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}A.

We report each Spearman correlation as a descriptive measure of how strongly
similarity tracks contact, not a p-value, because the pairs are not independent
(the same subtelomere recurs in many pairs).

Community-ordered contact matrices used observed/expected inter-chromosomal normalization \cite{hic3d_liebermanaiden2009,hic3d_imakaev2012}.

For the community-level contact analysis we classified each inter-chromosomal
arm pair as within-community or between-community using the arm-level Leiden
($k=15$) partition, and summarized the contrast as $B/W$, the mean contact of
between-community pairs divided by the mean contact of within-community pairs
(so $B/W<1$ indicates stronger within-community contact). %%% ("As a unique-sequence control for multi-mapping we repeated the calculation
%%% on the 100 kb immediately centromere-ward of each PHR, outside the called
%%% PHR; these non-PHR flanks show the same within-community enrichment.")
As a unique-sequence control for multi-mapping we repeated the calculation on
the 100\,kb immediately centromere-ward of each PHR, outside the called PHR and
over the same arm-pair set. We report both the $B/W$ contrast and the arm-level
Mantel $\rho$ for the control, because they answer different questions and, for
this comparison, rank the two window types oppositely: $B/W$ is a ratio of two
class means and is dominated by contact crossing the community boundary,
whereas $\rho$ is a rank correlation over all arm pairs and is invariant to a
background shared by both classes. In HG002 Hi-C at 50\,kb, $B/W$ is 0.0267 for
PHR windows and 0.0021 for the flanks while $\rho$ is 0.657 and 0.520; in CHM13
Hi-C the same comparison gives 0.0706 against 0.0567 and 0.656 against 0.522.
Because uniform placement of a multi-mapping read attenuates a specific contact
by about $1/k$ per end symmetrically across community classes, both PHR-window
statistics are biased towards the null, and the PHR-window enrichment they
report is a lower bound.

\subsection*{Population differentiation}

Hudson $F_{ST}$ was computed per arm and per superpopulation pair from the same allele
frequencies used for the community analyses, restricted to the ten arm/community blocks that
carry the signal, and summarised per pair with a block jackknife over arms (10 blocks) giving a
95\% interval. Subtelomeric values were compared with matched genome-wide continental Hudson
$F_{ST}$ for the same five superpopulation labels and the same metric (Bhatia et al.\ 2013,
\emph{Genome Research} 23:1514, Table 1; Patterson et al.\ 2012, \emph{Genetics} 192:1065). The
comparison is expressed as a difference and a ratio, and each pair is classified as equivalent,
elevated or depressed with respect to the matched baseline
(\texttt{scripts/popgen/matched\_fst\_d\_m6.py}). The baseline is the published genome-wide
expectation for these labels rather than a cohort-internal window sample; sampling matched
non-subtelomeric autosomal windows from the phased HPRC v2 VCF is the equivalent-length native
control and is noted as the remaining step. We do not build the population comparison on local-ancestry-deconvolved genotypes. HPRC
r2 provides local-ancestry calls (PCLAI) that are released in assembly, CHM13 and GRCh38
coordinates; in assembly coordinates they cover 83--100\% of the terminal 500 kb of each contig
we analysed, with window confidence of 885--1000 (lowest at the chr9q end), whereas the same
annotation projected onto CHM13 retains only about 55\% of a subtelomeric flank because the
pangenome-specific duplicated portion cannot be hosted there. Ancestry is therefore resolvable
over most of a flank but not entirely, and the pedigree individuals are not all covered (only
PAN027 is present in the r2 release), so the comparison is reported at arm resolution with
continental superpopulation labels and the standard Hudson estimator.

\subsection*{Mouse pipeline}

We repeated the entire pipeline on a non-human mammal, using the B6 and CAST T2T
assemblies \cite{Francis2025}. We extracted telomere-anchored flanks and called PHRs de novo over 1 Mb flanks. The stage-resolved
meiotic Hi-C is from inbred C57BL/6 (B6) spermatocytes \cite{Zuo2021}. Because
these mice are inbred and homozygous, the reads are single-haplotype and are not
phased into parental haplotypes; we mapped them to the combined B6+CAST T2T
reference, keeping multi-mapping reads exactly as in the human pipeline. For each meiotic
prophase stage we computed the Spearman correlation between length-normalized contact and
PHR Jaccard similarity over the 1{,}135 inter-chromosomal PHR pairs (1 Mb flanks, 20 kb
windows). The pairs share chromosome ends, so we kept pair resolution and handled the
dependence in the inference. The correlation is the slope of an ordinary least-squares
regression of rank-standardized contact on rank-standardized similarity; its variance is the
two-way cluster-robust estimate \cite{Cameron2011multiway} over the two arms of each pair,
with the arm pair as the intersection term (27 arms, 344 arm pairs). The null is a
permutation that shuffles the similarity of each PHR pair only among PHR pairs of the same
arm pair (10,000 permutations), so arm identity, distance to the telomere, the number of
pairs per arm pair and every contact value stay fixed. To compare stages we regressed the
difference of the standardized contacts of two stages on the shared similarity, which gives
the difference of the two correlations directly, with the same clustered variance and Holm
correction over the six stage contrasts. A bootstrap over the 27 chromosome ends (10,000
replicates) gives wider intervals and does not resolve the zygotene--pachytene contrast
(95\% CI $-0.131$ to $+0.043$); all other adjacent contrasts stay resolved. For display only,
we also averaged contact over the PHR pairs of each arm pair and computed an arm-level Mantel
test (344 arm pairs, 10,000 row-and-column permutations; $\rho = 0.40$, 0.58, 0.63 and 0.33
from leptotene to diplotene, all $p \leq 10^{-4}$). That summary discards the variance within
arm pairs and puts the peak at pachytene, so we do not use it for inference. At 50 kb
windows the four stages do not separate.
We also computed this correlation within each arm class, using only
inter-chromosomal pairs in which both PHRs lie on p-arms ($n = 722$) or both on
q-arms ($n = 42$).

\subsection*{Pedigree direct-alignment windows}

\emph{Assembly correction.} Before the comparisons we corrected the version 1.1 assemblies of all
four individuals, each with that individual's own reads only. We flagged 10\,kb bins where the
individual's HiFi depth fell to at most a quarter of the contig median, or where the HiFi reads
carried at least three single-nucleotide differences at depth at least 3, merged flagged bins
within 20\,kb and skipped groups lying mostly in centromeric satellite arrays. For each candidate we reassembled the candidate $\pm$400\,kb with hifiasm 0.25.0 from
the individual's HiFi reads, ONT reads or both, and took as replacement a contig that matched the
assembly exactly over at least 5\,kb on each side. We applied a replacement only when more of the
individual's HiFi reads and more of their ONT reads aligned better to it than to the assembled
sequence, and kept it only when it did not raise the number of assembly 21-mers absent from the
individual's Illumina reads (Merqury) within 1\,kb. We then polished the replaced pieces with the
individual's HiFi reads (bcftools haploid calls with quality at least 20). Two chromosome ends
needed separate fixes. We replaced the terminal 150,287\,bp of the father's chr9q haplotype 2 with a
contig assembled from his HiFi reads alone, joined at the end of a 120,784\,bp exact match to the
assembly, and polished it with his HiFi reads (one indel corrected). We also removed a 10,434\,bp tail without telomeric repeats beyond the telomere of the
daughter's maternal chr22q. In total we made 318 changes (316 reassembled pieces and the two end
fixes). The Merqury quality value rose in all eight haplotypes (range 56.83--60.78 before, 57.18--60.85
after) and the assembly 21-mers absent from the Illumina reads fell from 773,971 to 712,344. One
script rebuilds the four corrected assemblies from the released ones and reproduces them base for
base.

For each transmission (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed7}C) we aligned one child haplotype
(the query) against the combined two-haplotype assembly of the transmitting
parent (the target) \cite{Cechova2025}, both corrected. We cut each query chromosome into equal
pieces of at most 2\,Mb, because FastGA stopped the alignment chain short of some chromosome ends
when the query was a whole chromosome, aligned the pieces in eight groups of whole chromosomes with
sweepga 0.1.1~\cite{sweepga} (commit 02e7ed0d, with its bundled FastGA, frequency threshold 32,
many-to-many mappings, no scaffold jumps) and returned every alignment to chromosome coordinates.
When sweepga's conversion to PAF failed on a group, we reran the same FastGA call and converted its
output with ALNtoPAF on one thread, which gave output identical to sweepga's on a group where both ran (180,083
of 180,083 lines). We filtered to 18:18 mapping classes and summarized the
query in non-overlapping 2 kb windows, excluding centromeric windows, windows
without alignment support, and windows with inter-chromosomal alignment depth
above 100. For each retained window we computed impg 0.4.1 similarity and kept
the best same-chromosome or homologous parental match and the best
non-homologous parental match, assigning the window to the non-homologous class
only when the non-homologous match was the most similar. The identity margin of
a candidate is the amount by which its best non-homologous match exceeds
the best same-chromosome or homologous match across the tract.

\emph{Why a margin alone cannot identify a donor here.} At subtelomeric ends the competing sequences are
near-identical paralogues, so an exchange and a shared family member can both produce a non-homologous
winner. Two independent checks are therefore required, and both are reported. First, the seed-frequency
sensitivity: the candidate set entering the alignment depends on the FastGA frequency threshold, and on
the released assemblies we scanned thresholds 32, 48, 64, 96, 128, 192 and 255. The chr1p/chr5q candidate exists only at threshold
32 and disappears at 48, where the father's chr5q homologue reaches the last base of its contig at
identity 1.0000; the chr9q/chr3q candidate survives 32 and 48 but disappears by 96. Neither call is
invariant to the parameter that was chosen for tractability, which is why the alignment identity alone is
not used as evidence in this paper. Second, the assembly check: we corrected all four assemblies with each
individual's own reads, without any data from the other pedigree members (above), and rescanned every
transmission on the corrected assemblies.

\emph{Competitive similarity test.} Because a call can be lost or created simply by which candidates reach
the aligner, the definitive test offers all candidate ends simultaneously. We took the terminal 200\,kb of
the daughter's paternal chr9q and of every competing paternal end from the corrected assemblies (the
father's chr9q haplotypes 1 and 2, his chr3q, chr19q and chr1p, and his chr2p as a no-signal control,
with p ends reverse-complemented so that the telomere comes last), added the released chr9q haplotype 2,
placed all in one FASTA, aligned them all-against-all with sweepga (many-to-many mappings, no scaffold
jumps, FastGA frequency 5000 so that no candidate is filtered out), and scored them with impg 0.4.1
similarity in 2\,kb windows of the daughter's chr9q in graph mode (\texttt{-{}-gfa-engine poa}). For each
window and candidate we kept the best-scoring pair and averaged over the ten windows of the called tract
(\texttt{PAN027\#2\#chr9:136,168,000--136,188,000}) and over the other windows.

\emph{The similarity measure.} Each sequence is a path through the alignment graph, and the comparison is
made on the multiset of graph nodes that path traverses, weighted by node length, so a path that loops
through a repeat counts those bases more than once. On those multisets we report the dice coefficient, twice the shared content divided by the sum of the two lengths,
which we refer to throughout as \emph{graph dice}: the fraction of the combined sequence content of the
two paths that is shared. Two properties recommend it here. It is coverage-aware,
so a candidate that matches only part of a window is penalised, which is the question we are asking of
these ends. And it is the same overlap measure that defines the communities elsewhere in the paper, which
is reported as a Jaccard index; for the same node multisets the two are related exactly by
$D=2J/(1+J)$, a strictly monotone transform, so no ranking depends on which is quoted. The Mash-transformed \texttt{estimated.identity} emitted by the same command
in its faster sketch mode is not used: it is a function of the k-mer Jaccard rather than of the graph,
and it compresses differences between near-identical candidates. Candidate rankings are read directly from
the graph dice values, with every candidate present at once and no dependence on a frequency threshold.

The pairwise dot plots of Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed4} use permissive FastGA settings (seed
frequency 8192, chain start threshold 200, minimum chain coverage 70\%, minimum identity 0.6) to retain
short and repeat-rich matches that the scan threshold suppresses. We joined two
neighboring windows into one run only when their query and donor intervals were
directly adjacent, touching end to end with no gap, on the same donor
chromosome, haplotype and sequence; we did not bridge gaps or chain non-adjacent
windows. We drew runs of at least 10 kb with mean identity at least 0.95, shown
as the non-homologous (colored) and same-chromosome (gray) runs in
Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed7}C.

\emph{Chromosome-copy assignment.} Haplotype-resolved assemblies separate the two
copies of every chromosome but do not record which parent contributed each copy. For this
pedigree that assignment is per chromosome (for PAN028 it derives from imprinted loci, for
PAN027 from parental k-mers), and the released file names do not encode it; we therefore
resolved, chromosome by chromosome, the copy inherited from the transmitting parent by
identity to that parent's two haplotypes before comparison, and we assign a block of tens
of kilobases to a non-homologous source only when the most similar sequence in the
transmitting parent's genome lies on another chromosome (the exceptions being PAR1 and the
acrocentric short arms). Transmissions are
presented only where the donor genome is complete: both of PAN027's parents are sequenced,
whereas PAN028's father is not, so her paternal copy is excluded from the analysis.

\emph{Pedigree runs and Leiden communities.} We assigned each end of a drawn run to a chromosome arm by
its position relative to the active centromeric HOR arrays of its own contig (release annotation), and
each arm to its Leiden community. Of the 82 runs of the three transmissions (1,574\,kb), 80 join arms of
the same community (0.976, Wilson 95\% CI 0.915--0.993): the 78 runs between acrocentric short arms and
the two PAR1 runs. The two exceptions join interior sequence to acrocentric short arms, one in the chr3
centromere region between two active HOR arrays (18\,kb) and one in the chr1q pericentromeric region at
131.0\,Mb (10\,kb). Because the expected within-community fraction depends on how runs fall on arms,
we compared it with a null that permutes the donor arms over all runs and so keeps the per-arm run
counts on both sides (10,000 permutations). The null fraction has mean 0.929 (95\% interval
0.927--0.951), and 2 of the 10,000 permutations reach the observed fraction ($p = 0.0003$).

\emph{Statistical controls for the clustering statistics.} The arm-level
tree was assessed with a character-level bootstrap: 1,000 replicates resampling the 15,089
signal-bearing PHRs with replacement, recomputing the arm-level Jaccard matrix and rebuilding
neighbour-joining and UPGMA trees per replicate. Recovery is 100\% for the Xp/Yp and Xq/Yq
pairs and for 10p/18p, 54\% (neighbour-joining) to 88\% (UPGMA) for the acrocentric p-arm
group; the TIGHT\_q and 4q/10q groups are community assignments from the Leiden partition and
are not monophyletic clades in the arm-level tree, so clade support is not claimed for them.
$F_{ST}$ carries a block-jackknife 95\% interval and Mantel $\rho$ values carry bootstrap
intervals.

\emph{Assembly quality of the runs.} Besides the read-based correction of all four assemblies, we
counted Merqury error k-mers (assembly 21-mers absent from the person's Illumina reads) inside each
run, on the transmitted haplotype and on the donor haplotype. Outside PAR1 the 80 runs carry 1 such
k-mer on the transmitted side and none on the donor side, against 33.8 and 42.4 expected from each
haplotype's genome-wide rate. The two PAR1 runs carry 26 and 49 (2.4 and 5.5 expected).

\emph{How each retained call is supported.} PAR1 is an obligate exchange and serves as the positive
control. Across 138\,kb in two runs the daughter's paternal Xp matches her father's Yp at mean identity
0.99996 and 0.99991, and his Xp at 0.900 and 0.877. We do not retain the chr9q/chr3q terminus, because
it disappears once we correct the father's chr9q haplotype 2 (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed7}C). The
acrocentric runs recur across the short arms: the 12 transmitted acrocentric short arms with runs carry
1 to 30 runs each, from 1 to 4 donor short arms. Every run, with its arms, communities, identities and
Merqury counts, is listed in \texttt{supporting\_material/Fig5\_pedigree\_runs.tsv}.
